# ISO 19011:2018 Guidelines for Auditing Management Systems Handbook

**Version:** 2018 | **Provider:** ISO (International Organization for Standardization)
**Type:** Guidance (not certifiable) | **Applies to:** Auditors of any ISO management system
**Last Updated:** 2026-02-22

---

## How to Use This Handbook

This handbook is the practitioner's guide to ISO 19011:2018. It explains the
standard's guidance on audit programme management, conducting audits, and auditor
competence. Unlike the other ISO handbooks in this framework, ISO 19011 is a
**guideline standard** — it has no certifiable requirements. It is used as a
methodology reference by auditors and audit programme managers.

**This handbook IS:** A narrative walkthrough of audit programme management and
execution methodology per ISO 19011.

**This handbook is NOT:**
- A requirements standard (ISO 19011 uses "should" throughout, not "shall")
- A substitute for auditor training and experience
- Directly applicable to organizations being audited (it guides auditors, not auditees)

## 1. Framework Overview

### What ISO 19011:2018 Is

ISO 19011:2018 provides guidelines for auditing management systems, including audit
programme management, planning and conducting individual audits, and evaluating
auditor competence. It applies to all types of management system audits:
ISO 9001, ISO 14001, ISO 27001, ISO 13485, ISO 45001, ISO 42001, and others.

The 2018 edition (third edition) made important additions compared to ISO 19011:2011:

- **Risk-based approach (Clause 4.g):** Added as a new auditing principle
- **Remote auditing:** Explicit guidance on conducting audits using technology
  (video conferencing, remote document access, electronic sampling)
- **Updated Annex A:** Added specific competence guidelines for ISO 14001, ISO 45001,
  and updated other disciplines
- **Performance improvement:** Clearer guidance on using audit findings to drive
  improvement

### Relationship to Other Standards

ISO 19011 is the **methodology** standard. For organizations that want to become
accredited certification bodies, the requirements are in ISO/IEC 17021-1
(Requirements for bodies providing audit and certification of management systems),
supplemented by discipline-specific standards:

- ISO/IEC 27006 — Requirements for CB auditing ISMS (ISO 27001)
- ISO/IEC 42006 — Requirements for CB auditing AIMS (ISO 42001)
- IAF MD documents — Mandatory documents for accredited CBs

ISO 19011 provides the how-to guidance that practitioners apply within the framework
set by those requirements standards.

### Why Auditors Need ISO 19011

Auditors who understand and apply ISO 19011 guidance:
- Conduct more objective, evidence-based audits
- Identify more systemic root causes rather than symptoms
- Build more productive relationships with auditees
- Produce audit reports that drive meaningful improvement
- Meet competence requirements for certification body auditors

## 2. The Seven Auditing Principles (Clause 4)

The principles underpin all effective auditing. They are guidance for auditor
behavior, not organizational requirements.

### Principle 1: Integrity

Auditors shall be honest, diligent, responsible, and discreet. They should only
undertake audits where they have the competence to do so. Integrity is the
foundation of auditor credibility — an audit result is only as good as the trust
placed in the auditor who produced it.

**In practice:** Declare conflicts of interest before accepting an engagement.
Decline assignments where you lack technical competence. Never accept gifts or
hospitality that could create perceived bias.

### Principle 2: Fair Presentation

Audit findings, conclusions, and reports must truthfully and accurately reflect
audit activities. This includes reporting both conformities and nonconformities,
and clearly documenting any unresolved disagreements between the audit team
and auditee.

**In practice:** Do not soften findings to avoid conflict. If the auditee disputes
a finding, document the disagreement rather than removing the finding without
evidence that changes the conclusion.

### Principle 3: Due Professional Care

Apply diligence and sound judgment in proportion to the importance and complexity
of the audit. This includes investing adequate preparation time, using appropriate
sampling, and consulting technical experts when needed.

**In practice:** Do not rush audit preparation. A well-prepared auditor who has
reviewed documentation before arriving on-site is more effective than one who
reviews documents during the audit.

### Principle 4: Confidentiality

Treat all auditee information as confidential. Information gathered during an audit
should not be used for personal advantage, shared with competitors, or disclosed
without authorization.

**In practice:** Audit working papers should be stored securely. Electronic documents
shared for audit review should be destroyed or returned per agreement. Working papers
typically do not need to be retained beyond the audit report issuance.

### Principle 5: Independence

Auditors must be independent of the activity being audited. This means no line
management responsibility over the audited area and no financial interest in the
audit outcome. Independence prevents confirmation bias and ensures objective findings.

**In practice:** Internal auditors cannot audit their own department or processes
they directly manage. External auditors must declare all relevant relationships
with the auditee. For continuous improvement contexts, use structured self-assessment
rather than presenting it as an independent audit.

### Principle 6: Evidence-Based Approach

Audit conclusions must be based on verifiable evidence — not impressions,
assumptions, or auditee statements alone. Since audits sample available information,
sampling must be designed thoughtfully to provide meaningful conclusions.

**In practice:** Corroborate claims with records. If an auditee says "we always
do X," verify with records. If records are unavailable, note the absence of
evidence as a finding or observation.

### Principle 7: Risk-Based Approach (Added in 2018)

Audit resources should be directed toward areas of highest risk and significance.
This influences planning (how much time to allocate per process), sampling (which
records to select), and reporting (which findings to emphasize).

**In practice:** A new process with no audit history deserves more scrutiny than
a mature, well-documented process with clean audit history. Allocate audit time
accordingly.

## 3. Managing an Audit Programme (Clause 5)

### Programme vs. Individual Audit

The **audit programme** is the organizational infrastructure for ongoing audit activity
— it defines annual scheduling, resource allocation, methodology, and performance
measurement. Individual audits are executions within the programme.

### Programme Objectives

An effective audit programme should be designed to:

- Verify conformity with management system requirements
- Evaluate QMS/ISMS/AIMS effectiveness (not just compliance)
- Identify improvement opportunities
- Satisfy regulatory, customer, or stakeholder audit requirements
- Support management decision-making with objective performance data

### Factors Influencing Programme Extent

The depth and breadth of the audit programme should reflect:

- **Organizational complexity:** More processes, sites, and people require a larger
  programme
- **Regulatory requirements:** Regulated industries (medical devices, food safety,
  financial services) may have mandated audit frequencies
- **Risk and significance:** Higher-risk processes deserve more frequent audit
- **Previous audit results:** Areas with recurring nonconformities or where root
  cause is unclear deserve more attention
- **Changes:** New products, processes, facilities, or personnel increase audit
  priorities
- **Management system maturity:** Immature systems need more frequent assessment
  than well-established ones

### Programme Management Responsibilities

The person managing the audit programme should:

1. Establish programme objectives based on organizational and regulatory needs
2. Develop the annual audit schedule with appropriate frequency per area/clause
3. Select and assign qualified audit teams
4. Ensure auditors are competent and maintain that competence
5. Review programme results and continuously improve the programme
6. Report programme performance to top management
7. Maintain audit programme records

### Common Programme Failures

- **Scheduling only — no objectives:** Programme is a list of audits without
  defined purpose or expected outcomes
- **Risk-blind scheduling:** Same frequency for all processes regardless of risk
- **Auditor pool too small:** Same 1-2 auditors cover everything, creating independence
  and fatigue issues
- **No programme review:** Programme runs unchanged year after year without
  evaluating effectiveness

## 4. Performing an Audit (Clause 6)

### Phase 1: Initiating

Before fieldwork begins, the audit team leader should:

- Confirm audit objectives, scope, and criteria with the audit client
- Assess audit feasibility (sufficient information, auditee cooperation, adequate
  time and resources)
- Establish initial contact with the auditee to confirm logistics
- Verify audit team member independence from the areas to be audited

### Phase 2: Preparing

Preparation is where audit effectiveness is largely determined:

**Document review:** Request and review QMS documentation, previous audit reports,
and corrective action status before the on-site visit. This allows:
- Identification of areas requiring additional scrutiny
- Development of targeted checklists
- Assessment of documentation adequacy before fieldwork

**Audit plan:** A written plan should cover:
- Audit objectives, scope, and criteria
- Date and location of on-site activities
- Time allocation per process/clause
- Audit team assignments (who audits what)
- Methods (interviews, observation, document review, data analysis)
- Reporting and closing meeting arrangements

**Work documents:** Checklists, sampling plans, and evidence collection forms.
Checklists are tools, not scripts — they guide the auditor but should not prevent
following audit trails discovered during fieldwork.

### Phase 3: On-Site Activities

**Opening meeting** sets the tone for the entire audit. A professional opening meeting:
- Introduces the audit team and auditee participants
- Confirms the audit plan and objectives
- Explains the audit process (evidence-based, sampling approach)
- Confirms confidentiality obligations
- Addresses logistics (guides, access to facilities, interview scheduling)
- Invites questions from the auditee

**Collecting evidence** uses multiple methods:

| Method | Best For |
|--------|----------|
| Interviews | Understanding intent, procedures, and culture |
| Observation | Verifying that procedures are followed in practice |
| Document review | Confirming documentation completeness and control |
| Record review | Verifying that activities were performed as required |
| Data analysis | Identifying trends, calculating metrics, sampling |

**Audit trails:** Follow the evidence where it leads. If an interview reveals
an unexpected issue outside the planned scope, note it and discuss with the
team leader. Some of the most significant findings come from unexpected audit trails.

**Generating findings:** Each finding should state:
- The requirement that was not met (specific clause reference)
- The objective evidence observed (facts, not opinions)
- The significance (major nonconformity, minor nonconformity, or observation)

**Finding classification:**
- **Major nonconformity:** Systematic or significant absence of a required element,
  or multiple minor nonconformities in the same area indicating system breakdown
- **Minor nonconformity:** Isolated failure to meet a specific requirement
- **Observation/Opportunity for improvement:** Not a requirement breach, but a
  practice that could be improved

**Closing meeting** presents all findings to the auditee. Best practices:
- Present facts, not judgments or recommendations (unless specifically within scope)
- Allow auditee to respond and ask questions
- Clarify timelines for corrective action response
- Confirm distribution of the audit report

### Phase 4: Reporting

The audit report is the formal record of the audit. Content should include:

- Audit objectives, scope, criteria, and dates
- Audit team composition
- Summary of audit activities
- Findings with evidence (nonconformities, observations)
- Audit conclusions (overall system conformity assessment)
- Unresolved issues or disagreements
- Recommendations (if within audit scope)

**Timeliness:** Issue the report within the agreed timeframe — typically 5-10
business days after the closing meeting. Delayed reports reduce the value of the
audit findings.

### Phase 5: Follow-Up

Audit follow-up verifies that:

1. The auditee has submitted a corrective action plan within the agreed timeframe
2. Corrective actions address the root cause, not just the symptom
3. Actions have been implemented
4. Actions were effective (the problem has not recurred)

Effectiveness verification is critical and frequently missed. Simply closing a
CAPA after "action implemented" without checking whether the problem actually
stopped is not effective follow-up.

## 5. Auditor Competence (Clause 7)

### Competence Framework

Auditor competence combines:

**Knowledge (what they know):**
- Audit principles and techniques (this standard)
- The management system standard(s) being audited
- Applicable regulatory requirements
- Sector-specific technical knowledge (processes, products, industry terminology)

**Skills (what they can do):**
- Plan and conduct interviews effectively
- Collect and analyze evidence systematically
- Identify root causes, not just symptoms
- Write clear, factual findings
- Manage time during on-site activities
- Remain objective under pressure

**Personal attributes (how they behave):**
- Ethical and honest (Principle 1)
- Open-minded and receptive to new information
- Diplomatic (professional relationships despite adversarial findings)
- Observant (notices what is happening, not just what is shown)
- Tenacious (follows audit trails to conclusion)
- Decisive (makes findings based on available evidence)
- Self-reliant (works independently with minimal supervision)
- Culturally aware (adapts to organizational and national culture)

### Auditor Evaluation Methods

ISO 19011 recommends using two or more evaluation methods:

| Method | What It Assesses |
|--------|-----------------|
| Records review | Education, experience, training credentials |
| Feedback from referees | Past performance, professional reputation |
| Interviews | Knowledge, judgment, communication skills |
| Observation during audit | Practical competence in real audit conditions |
| Role play/simulation | Response to challenging scenarios |
| Written examination | Technical knowledge of standards |
| Post-audit review | Performance against objectives |

### Auditor Qualification Pathways

While ISO 19011 does not specify certification requirements, common pathways include:

- **CQI/IRCA Certified Auditor:** Recognized in ISO 9001, ISO 14001, ISO 27001,
  ISO 13485 auditing
- **Exemplar Global:** Formerly RABQSA, provides auditor certification across
  management system disciplines
- **Sector-specific qualifications:** IATF 16949 approved auditor lists, FDA
  ORA inspector training for medical devices

### Maintaining Auditor Competence

An auditor who does not conduct regular audits loses practical competence. ISO 19011
guidance implies minimum continuing activity of:
- At least 2 complete audits per year to maintain practical skills
- Continuing professional development: training on standard updates, regulatory
  changes, new audit techniques
- Annual review of auditor performance and CPD records

## 6. Remote Auditing (2018 Addition)

The 2018 edition added explicit guidance on remote auditing techniques, which
became essential practice from 2020 onward.

**When remote auditing is appropriate:**
- Document review stages of audits
- Follow-up on corrective actions (document verification)
- Interviews with management and process owners
- Review of electronic records and data

**When remote auditing is inadequate:**
- Observation of physical production processes
- Verification of physical environmental conditions (cleanrooms, calibration labels)
- Assessment of workplace culture and behavior
- Sampling of physical materials or products

**Remote auditing considerations:**
- Technology reliability must be confirmed in advance
- Document security (screen sharing vs. uploads)
- Interview fatigue is greater via video — schedule breaks
- Time zones for multinational audits
- Confidentiality of electronic information shared during remote sessions

### Role in Compliance Assessments

Within the IA compliance skill, ISO 19011 guidance is applied when:

1. **Conducting compliance assessments** — The assessment methodology follows
   ISO 19011 principles: evidence-based, independent, fair presentation

3. **Training internal auditors** — Competence requirements in Clause 7
4. **Evaluating audit programme effectiveness** — Programme monitoring in Clause 5.5
